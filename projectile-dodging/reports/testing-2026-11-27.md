# Dodging tests of 27 November: four problems, what causes them, what would fix them

Data: the 66 recordings Pupper saved between 16:59 and 17:51 (50 armed dodges, 2 dry-run, 14 "seen, no dodge"), with every camera frame, the node's trace and the IMU. Copied off the Pupper and replayed on the Mac with the code and calibration it was running (commit 6844479). Fixes were tried in three ways:

- **Simulation** of throws at the camera rates measured today: fisheye 19 a second, 72 ms old when used; rear cameras 17 a second, 55 ms.
- **Replay** of all 405 recordings kept so far, to count false dodges.
- **MuJoCo physics** with the walking policy, for the falls.

Nothing below has been changed in the code or run on the robot.

## 1. A ball thrown in a high arc goes out of sight and is not detected

**What the cameras can see.** Neither rear camera is tilted, and the top of what they see is low:

| Ball comes from | Highest it can be and still be seen |
|---|---|
| Straight ahead, front quarters | 77° or more (fisheye) |
| Sides (90°) | about 39° (rear camera) |
| Rear quarters (135°) | about 41° |
| Straight behind | about 28° |

From 2–3 m, a lob that peaks a metre above the hand climbs past 40° within about 0.1–0.2 s. A ball on course to hit the robot climbs steadily in the picture right up to impact, so once it leaves through the top it never comes back.

**Why there is no recording of the misses.** Pupper saves a "seen" recording only for a ball it saw at least five times. A ball that leaves the top after two to four looks leaves nothing behind. That fits what you saw: the throw was not detected at all.

**Simulated dodge rate at today's camera rates, by direction and arc height.** 24 throws per cell. The column headings are how high the ball rises above the hand.

| Ball comes from | 0.4 m | 0.8 m | 1.2 m | 1.6 m | 2.0 m |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Straight ahead | 83 % | 100 % | 100 % | 100 % | 100 % |
| Front, 45° | 88 % | 100 % | 100 % | 100 % | 100 % |
| **Side, 90°** | 88 % | 88 % | **75 %** | **50 %** | **62 %** |
| Rear, 135° | 96 % | 100 % | 100 % | 88 % | 92 % |
| Straight behind | 100 % | 100 % | 96 % | 100 % | 100 % |

High lobs from the **sides** are the weak spot. There the fisheye cannot see them at all: 90° is outside its 84°. Only one rear camera sees the ball there, and only up to about 39°. The rule that lets the node decide on two looks applies only within 30° of straight behind.

**What was tried:**

| Fix | High lobs from the side | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Two-look rule extended to the sides (`two_look_min_bearing_deg` 75, `unseen_fire_min_bearing_deg` 75) | 72 → 94 % | Simulated false dodges 3 → 9 of 135 (balls lifted out of a basket, balls tossed up and caught). One more labelled false dodge in the recordings (205037). Brings back 205123, a ball handled at 95° |
| The same, needing a 6° climb (`two_look_min_climb_deg` 6) | 72 → 94 % | Simulated false dodges 3 → 4 of 135. Still the extra false dodge at 205037 |
| Rear cameras at 30 a second | 72 → 98 % (rear quarters 95 → 100 %) | None in simulation. Needs the processing time per picture roughly halved, see below |
| Rear cameras searched at half size (a quarter of the pixels), about 35 a second | 72 → 98 % (rear quarters 95 → 100 %) | The ball is half as many pixels across, which costs some range. Already counted in this result |
| Another picture mode on the rear cameras | — | Rejected. The sensor is 1920×1200. The 4:3 modes see higher but are cropped at the sides, which would open a 4° gap straight behind (the cameras overlap by only 5° there). Full 1920×1200 gains only about 3° at the top |

**Half-size search, in more detail.** Decoding a rear JPEG at half size took 35 % less time on the Mac (5.4 → 3.6 ms), and the search would cover a quarter of the pixels. On the Pi a rear picture now takes about 15 ms to decode and 20–28 ms to search, so this should roughly double each rear camera's rate. That estimate is unmeasured: measure it on the Pi with `tools/bench_detect.py` before relying on it.

**Recommended:**
1. Half-size search for the rear cameras, if the Pi measurement confirms the speed-up.
2. Meanwhile, from the sides throw no higher than about a metre above your hand.
3. Have the node also record a climbing ball that leaves the top of a picture after two to four looks, so these misses show up in the data.

## 2. The fisheye camera is sometimes slow

**Measured.** Age of each picture by the time the node had finished with it:

| | Median | 90 % of pictures | 99 % | Worst |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fisheye | 72 ms | 123 ms | 184 ms | 1.6 s, once, at 16:59 |
| Rear cameras | 52–59 ms | 84–100 ms | 131–155 ms | about 0.2 s |

21 % of fisheye pictures were more than 100 ms old when used, against 5–10 % for the rear cameras. While the robot is dodging, the fisheye's median rises from 65 to 84 ms.

**Where the time goes, for a fisheye picture:**
- About 35 ms before the node even receives it. The course's camera node encodes every 1400×1050 picture as a JPEG in software, then sends it over ROS. The rear cameras skip this step: they send JPEGs from the camera itself.
- 15 ms to decode it.
- 17 ms to search it on average: 14 ms when nothing ball-coloured is in view, 26 ms when something is.
- **54 % of fisheye pictures contain something ball-coloured** (ceiling lights, warm-coloured objects). The fisheye's colour calibration on the Pupper is still the one that learned the hand (hue 8–27). The node ignores it, so the fisheye runs on generic colour limits. Pictures with clutter are 18 ms older on average.

**How much it matters (simulated, front cone, all arc heights):**

| Fisheye | Front cone dodged |
|---|---|
| As measured (72 ms, 19 a second) | 79 % |
| 40 ms old | 84 % |
| 30 a second | 86 % |
| During a 120 ms slow spell | 68 % |

**Options, cheapest first:**
1. **Redo the fisheye colour calibration** (DEPLOY.md step C2, the ball held by your fingertips). Fewer clutter blobs should save about 10 ms on average and remove most of the slow tail. It also gives fewer false tracks in front.
2. ~~`bash robot/start.sh --fisheye-quality 70`~~. **Tested later and rejected** (see "Changes made in the evening" below): in replay it lost dodges.
3. Pin each program to its own processor core: the fisheye's process on one, the camera node on another. This should cut the slow spells caused by four programs sharing four cores. Untested.
4. Have the camera node crop to the lit disc of the lens. Only about 40 % of the 1400×1050 frame is picture. This needs a change to the course camera node's settings: try it, measure it.
5. Not recommended: a smaller fisheye picture. At 2.5 m the ball is only 6–8 px across, and halving that loses it.

## 3. A throw from behind was not detected or acted on

**What the recordings show.** I went through every "seen, no dodge" recording and every throw the node held back today. None is a real throw from behind that went ignored:
- **171349** and **174657** ("seen" from behind): someone walking past carrying a ball, and someone picking balls up far away. Correctly ignored.
- **171116**: a threat straight behind, held back 1.07 s after a dodge. It was the dodged ball bouncing back up right behind the robot. Correctly ignored.

**What does cause misses and late dodges from behind:**
- **The lock-out after a dodge.** After each dodge, the node ignores every ball until it has stopped and steadied, plus 1 s more: about 2.2 s in all. You threw quickly: 22 of the 52 gaps between dodges were under 4 s, and the shortest was 2.0 s. Four throws today arrived inside the lock-out and were dodged late:

  | Dodge | Time left at decision | Time needed |
  |---|---|---|
  | 174500 | 0.30 s | 0.54 s |
  | 174840 | 0.11 s | 0.12 s |
  | 174842 | 0.18 s | 0.67 s |
  | 175042 | 0.50 s | 0.56 s |

  A ball arriving entirely within the lock-out is not dodged at all. That is the most likely explanation for what you saw.
- **High lobs from behind** (part 1) leave the picture within about 0.1 s and leave no recording.
- **Rear dodges are often late.** 18 of 28 were decided with less time left than the planned move needs: a median of 0.40 s left against 0.50 s needed.

**Fix evaluated: lock out only the balls that were already in the air at the dodge.** A new throw is let through. Anything first seen within 0.9 m of the robot, such as a bounce, still waits.

| Second ball thrown this long after the first | 1.0 s | 1.5 s | 2.0 s | 2.5 s | 3.0 s |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Now | 28 % | 0 % | 90 % | 90 % | 95 % |
| Lock-out per ball | 90 % | 98 % | 98 % | 90 % | 95 % |
| Lock-out shortened to 0.3 s | 48 % | 98 % | 98 % | 90 % | 95 % |

The table shows the share of second balls dodged in simulation; the second ball is aimed at where Pupper is when it is thrown.

Replayed over all 405 recordings, the per-ball lock-out adds one dodge and no false ones. The extra dodge is the real throw in 171142, now dodged earlier. Shortening the lock-out instead is simpler, but it lets bounces through; the per-ball version keeps them out.

**Already done.** The dodging session committed this fix while this report was being written: commit 5109ca3, "a second ball soon after the first is dodged too". Only the tracks already dodged wait. A different ball first seen at least 1.2 m away (`fsm.second_ball_min_range_m`) can start a dodge once the body is level. Its own simulation gives 85–93 %. It is on GitHub but not yet on the Pupper, and it has not been run on the robot.

Also still to do: measure the rear lenses (DEPLOY.md step C4). In simulation, an unmeasured lens 4° off at its edge cuts straight-behind dodges from 97 % to about 60 %.

## 4. Dodges tip the robot over

**Measured, 50 armed dodges on the wood floor:**
- **Two falls.**
  - 174506: a backward-right diagonal, and the ball also hit it on top (your label).
  - 175101: sideways to the right. The robot turned 31° during the move, the most of any dodge, and rolled over about 0.6 s after its command had reached zero.

  The next recording (175107) starts with the robot already on its side.
- **The tilt peaks as the robot stops,** 0 to 0.3 s after the burst ends. The command already eases to zero over 0.2 s.
- **Diagonals tip about twice as much as straight moves:**

  | Move | Dodges | Median peak tilt | 12° or more |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | Diagonal | 27 | 11° | 11 |
  | Straight sideways | 20 | 6.5° | 2 |

  Stopping from a forward diagonal pitched the body at up to 214°/s (175026, 25°).

**Physics simulation of the walking policy.**

*Setup.*
- The walking controller (gemini_walk) runs the course's `default_policy.json`. The harness runs it in MuJoCo at its trained 50 Hz.
- The controller's observation layout, command path and gait reference were ported from `neural_controller.cpp`. The gait reference matches the course's reference data to 6e-8.
- Before any dodge tests, it was checked on plain commands: it stands still, and it walks at the commanded speed to within about 10 %.

*Main results.*
- About 26,000 simulated dodges were run. Full report: [falls-mujoco/REPORT.md](falls-mujoco/REPORT.md).
- **It reproduces the robot's pattern:** diagonals tilt more than sideways moves, the peak comes just after the burst, and forward-diagonal pitch rates reach about 214°/s.
- The levels match only if the delay from command to motors is set to 40–50 ms (or the contacts made firmer). The policy was trained with about 16 ms. At 60 ms and more, falls appear in the simulation, 0–0.5 s after the command reaches zero, which is the real timing.
- **It never reproduced the sideways fall** (0 falls in 5,080 simulated sideways dodges). The robot's 31° turn during 175101 is outside what the model does, so something it lacks (a foot catching, a motor dropping out) is the likely cause.

**The biggest effect: the policy changes gait at 0.5 m/s forward.** At a forward command of 0.5 m/s or more, the controller switches to a faster gait (`gallop_speed` 0.5 in the policy file; three steps a second or more). Every forward dodge shipped today is in that gait: 0.8 m/s diagonals, 0.8–1.0 m/s straight ahead.

| Forward command | Median peak tilt | 12° or more | Distance at 0.5 s |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.49 m/s | 7° | 5 % | 0.12 m |
| 0.51 m/s | 13–18° | 70–95 % | 0.09–0.10 m |

So the faster gait tips much more and does not even travel further in the first half second. Backward is gentler: −0.8 m/s gives 0.17 m by 0.5 s at about 9°.

**Options, with simulated results.** Delay 40–50 ms; median peak tilt, share of dodges reaching 12° or more, sideways distance at 0.5 s.

| Change | Diagonal dodges | Straight forward or back | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| As shipped | 12.5° / 51 % / 0.04 m | 14.0° / 62 % | |
| **Diagonal replaced by straight sideways (0, ±0.5)** | **6.5° / 4 % / 0.09 m** | | Best. Half the tilt and twice the sideways distance |
| **Forward speed capped at 0.45 m/s** | **7.0° / 8 %** | **6.9° / 5 %** | Best for forward moves, at no cost in distance |
| Ease out over 0.4 or 0.6 s | 13.8–14.1° / about 60 % | 16.7° / 72 % | Worse: the fast gait runs longer |
| Ease in over 0.08–0.10 s | 11.0–11.8° / 42–49 % | | Little help, and 20–30 % less distance at 0.35 s |
| Diagonal speeds × 0.6 | 10.7° / 39 % | | Some help |
| Caps 0.8 forward and 0.4 sideways | unchanged | unchanged | No help: 0.8 is still the fast gait |
| Second dodge 1.5–2.5 s later | 11.9° / 49 % | 14.2° / 62 % | Little change. At 0.6–1.2 s apart, 2–12 % fell |

**What the robot's own data agree with.** Real diagonals tipped about twice as much as straight sideways moves, so replacing diagonals is backed by both the robot and the simulation. The forward-speed effect is not clear in today's data: forward and backward diagonals had similar median peaks (11.4° and 10.8°). Treat the 0.45 m/s cap as likely but unproven until it is tried on the robot.

**The sideways distance is short either way.** Straight sideways at the 0.5 m/s limit gives only about 0.09–0.1 m by 0.5 s after the decision. Clearing a ball aimed at the robot's middle needs more, so these changes trade a little dodge success for not falling. Check that with the dodge simulation before changing anything. An earlier decision helps, and so does backing away where the geometry allows it (0.17 m by 0.5 s).

**Recommended, in this order:**
1. Use straight sideways moves instead of diagonals.
2. Cap forward dodge speed at 0.45 m/s. Lowering speeds is allowed.
3. Leave the ease-out at 0.2 s.
4. Measure the delay from command to motors on the robot. The simulation matches the robot only at 40–50 ms and starts falling at 60 ms. The `ros2_control_node` runs at normal priority, not real-time.
5. Look at why the robot turned 31° in 175101. The recorded joint data and turn command for that dodge would show it.

**Reinforcement learning** is the thorough fix, but it should come after these. It means training a walking policy in pupper-mjlab on what dodging asks of it: sudden steps to full sideways speed, hard stops, a second dodge before the robot has settled, pushes and slippery floors. That is days of training plus testing on the robot. A new policy counts as a new trick, so it needs a TA's OK before its first run. The settings changes above get most of the benefit in an afternoon, and this harness can test any trained policy before it goes near the robot.

## Changes made in the evening of 27 November

Everything below was checked on the Mac only: replays of the recordings, the dodge simulation and the MuJoCo harness. None of it has run on the robot. What is left to do at the robot is in [../../improvements.md](../../improvements.md).

**Two robot models in the dodge simulation.** The simulation's robot answers a command through one lag of 0.2 s, whatever the command. In MuJoCo, the walking policy's fast forward gait (0.5 m/s and up) covers 0.09–0.10 m in the first half second whatever the speed. The lag model credits a 1.0 m/s command with 2.7 times that. `dodge.sim.mujoco_response` is a second model, fitted to the MuJoCo speed scan. It matches the MuJoCo distances at 0.5 s to within about 0.01 m for every command tested: forward 0.45 and 0.51–1.0, backward 0.45 and 0.8, sideways 0.4 and 0.5. Below, "MuJoCo-fitted" means the dodge simulation with that model and a 0.105 s actuation delay. Neither model is measured on the robot (step F).

### Dodge moves (config/dodge.yaml `escape:`)

| Setting | Before | Now |
|---|---|---|
| `vx_forward` | 1.0 | 0.45: below the policy's fast-gait switch at 0.5 |
| `diagonals` (new) | (always) | false: one axis at a time, whichever carries the robot away faster |
| `max_duration_s` | 0.8 | 0.45 |
| `side_choice_sigma_k` (new) | 0 | 1.5: when it is unsure which side the ball passes, the faster move wins |
| `vx_backward`, `vy`, `ramp_out_s` | 0.8, 0.5, 0.2 | unchanged |

Why each change:
- **Diagonals.** At trot speed, (±0.45, 0.5), they were no better in MuJoCo than one-axis moves. Over 0.8 s, 15–70 % of runs reached 12°, depending on the delay.
- **Burst cap.** Backing away at 0.8 m/s is in the policy's fast backward gait. MuJoCo, 50 ms delay, share of runs reaching 12°: 27 % for a 0.31 s burst, 40 % for 0.45 s, 70 % for 0.8 s. The new planner backs away in about 45 % of dodges, and its uncapped bursts had a median of 0.71 s. Capping at 0.45 s costs nothing in the dodge simulation. Backing away at 0.45 m/s instead tips far less (0–10 % at 40–50 ms) but dodges 6 points fewer.
- **Side choice.** With forward slow, the shorter side is often the slower move. A ball 60° ahead-right, aimed dead centre but estimated 9 cm off it, got a forward step and hit. Backing away would have cleared it by 11 cm.

MuJoCo, for the moves now used (median peak tilt / share reaching 12°; 30 runs each):

| Move (0.45 s) | 40 ms | 50 ms | 60 ms | Firm contacts |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sideways 0.5 | 6.5–7.4° / 0–3 % | 6.5–7.9° / 0–13 % | 7.5–9.0° / 7–20 % | 6.8–7.4° / 0–3 % |
| Forward 0.45 | 8.6° / 0 % | 9.7° / 17 % | 10.5° / 30 % | 9.2° / 30 % |
| Backward 0.8 | 8.6° / 10 % | 11.3° / 40 % | 16.1° / 80 % | 12.2° / 53 % |
| *Before: diagonals as shipped* | *12.5° / 51 % at 40–50 ms* | | | |

Backing away at 0.8 is still the riskiest move. On the robot on the 27th, its two straight backward dodges peaked at 5.4° and 7.1°. If backward dodges tip on the robot, set `vx_backward: 0.45`. That keeps every move at trot speed, for about 6 points fewer dodges in simulation. A second dodge 0.6–1.2 s after the first added no tilt to sideways moves.

Dodge simulation, 672 throws aimed at the robot, from 7 directions and 4 arc heights:

| | MuJoCo-fitted | Lag model |
|---|---|---|
| As shipped | 59.5 % | 74.0 % |
| Now | 61.3 % | 69.5 % |
| Now, but `vx_backward` 0.45 | 55.2 % | 63.4 % |

The lag model favours the old moves because it lets the fast forward gait cover ground that in MuJoCo it does not. Replayed over all 405 recordings, the dodge decisions are the same as before for 404. The other one (20261126-205520-seen) gets a second dodge. False dodges: 2 of 28 labelled, as before. In the replay tool's own simulated lobs, which use the lag model, 7 of 60 are not cleared, against 1 of 60 before.

### Balls that climb out of the top of a rear picture are recorded (`fsm.report_left_top`)

A rear-camera track that shows two to four looks climbing like a lob, its last look in the top strip of the picture, is reported as a `throw_seen` event with `left_top: true` once it is gone without a dodge. The recorder then keeps it. In the dodge simulation:
- High side lobs (1.2–2.0 m apex) that were not dodged are now reported 8 of 9 times and 11 of 12 times. The five-look rule reported 1 of each.
- No ball was reported twice, and none that led to a dodge.
- Balls lifted by hand near the robot may also be recorded. That costs SD card space (the 4 s gap between recordings and the 1.5 GB floor still apply), never a dodge.

### Half-size rear search: built, left off (`cameras.<name>.search_scale`)

`search_scale: 2` decodes each rear JPEG at half size and searches it there:
- The size correction (`size_offset_px`) now scales with the picture.
- The camera's mask is shrunk to fit; before, a mask of the wrong size was silently ignored.
- Ball positions agree with full size to 0.9 px. Ranges agree to a median of 1.00 (left) and 1.03 (right), interquartile range ±3–5 %.
- On the Mac it took 27–36 % less time per picture.

But replayed over the 66 recordings of the 27th, it changed the outcome of 13: 8 dodges lost, 5 gained. And 9 recordings got a second dodge, against 2 at full size. The extra first dodges came 1–2 s before the real one, on something 3–4 m behind or beside the robot. Scaling the detector's pixel thresholds with the picture made it no better (8 lost, 5 gained), and its shape tests then turned the ball away. Those recordings cannot show what the faster rate gains, but the accuracy cost is real. It stays at `search_scale: 1` until the Pi measurement and a fix for those far clutter dodges. `tools/bench_detect.py --full-decode --search-scale 2` measures it.

### Fisheye JPEG quality 70: rejected

Every fisheye picture of the 66 recordings was re-encoded at 85 and at 70 with the same encoder, then replayed:
- At 70, 14 recordings came out differently: 7 fewer dodges, 6 recordings lost their dodge entirely, and several dodges came 0.15–0.4 s later.
- There were 12 % more clutter blobs.
- The pictures were 23 % smaller, and encoding took no less time.

The default stays at 85.
